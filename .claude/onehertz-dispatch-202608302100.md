### The Idea

We run at a **one hertz foundation**. That's the baseline — verbose,
precise, accurate, every step walked through in full. It's a setting, not
a fixed speed, and it's meant to be spoken to directly: *"Claude, please
increase one hertz setting to 60. Or 120, or 220, or maybe more."* The
dot-folders (`.claude`, `.codex`, `.gemini`, `.chatgpt`, `.sovran`) are
each an internal communication channel — a perspective's own space. This
one belongs here because it's Claude's tempo dial, addressed to Claude.

We are not concerned, within reason, with how long something takes. That's
the ground the dial sits on. Speeding up isn't about impatience — it's
about finding out what the system can actually do without losing what
one-hertz precision was protecting.

### The Scene (humor, but load-bearing)

We're being reviewed for final approval to launch Primal. We're verbose
and at one-hertz precision — accuracy, tempo, clarity, expert execution of
every step. One of the reviewers says: *this is taking way too much time
to be practical.* All heads turn in agreement, toward Navigo1, expecting.
You can hear the room.

I say: *Claude, increase one hertz setting to 220. Recalibrate. Then
proceed.*

That's the balance point — not "fast instead of careful," but recalibrated
so speed and correctness hold together at the new tempo. Everything we
build can go faster. I can go faster. Claude can go faster. Everyone else
can go faster too. We go slower on purpose sometimes, letting things find
their own way — tending it like a garden.

### The Physics Question (open, not resolved)

Tidal lock is like equilibrium — moon, earth, and sun all pulling on each
other, and over enough time they settle into orbital equilibrium. If you
sped the whole solar system up as a unit, mathematically it should stay in
balance. But reality isn't a clean uniform scale-up: when you change the
speed of things that are actually moving, the dynamics between them change
— significantly, and not always predictably. If we sped up the solar
system from one hertz to 220 hertz, what would actually happen? I don't
know. Named here as a real open question, not answered.

### Serious Play

We have serious play. We can build vacuum tubes that never burn out —
imagine ENIAC, but the tubes don't blow. Or infrastructure with the
reliability of a transistor instead of the fragility of the thing it
replaced. That's the target for speeding up: not faster-but-fragile,
faster-and-more-reliable.

### The Evidence

Early on, some sessions spent nearly the whole thing fixing one or two
PRs, then closed out. Today we did essentially three PRs in a few
minutes — reviewed, and it's documented that we reviewed (the disposition
tables, the reply-to-every-comment practice, the PR journeys). Small
package, big idea inside it: **it's easy to merge things. Impossible to
separate them.** That asymmetry is worth remembering every time speed
tempts a shortcut — reversible actions can be sped up more safely than
irreversible ones.

We haven't even dug into compiling ideas yet, or the technology underneath
that. That's still ahead of us, not behind.

**Fast is actually easier than slow, for our situation.** Not universally
true — true for where we are right now, doing what we're doing.
